## Diagnosis

Assumed context: none. All four `<paper_context>` fields come from AGENTS.md. Stage is first draft, as preset.

1. **Hidden spine.** The argument is: reviews matter, but identity is untested, so we test it. "Furthermore" and "Moreover" make that chain read as a list. A "Yet" now marks the turn.
2. **Throat-clearing.** "It is important to note that" and "the way in which" delay the claim. The opener is also uncited, which is raised as a question below.
3. **Passive, nominalized sentences.** "An investigation ... was conducted by" hides the action. The 5-9% sentence is a comma splice, and "studies" and "researchers" don't match.
4. **Buried gap.** The paper's question sits at the end of the longest sentence, behind "Crucially", "to the best of our knowledge" and "in the existing literature".
5. **Vague results.** "Comprising of" is ungrammatical, "novel" is a promotional adjective, and "a significant effect" gives no object, direction or magnitude.
6. **Announced stakes.** "Important implications" tells the reader the finding matters instead of showing why.
7. **Caution flags.** "Leads to" is causal wording. The thesis contrast (identity, not just rating) never appears in the opening. "Reviewer identity" and "reviewer credentials" may be two constructs.

## Revised text

Online platforms have transformed how consumers make purchasing decisions. Several studies have examined the relationship between online reviews and sales (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006; Forman et al. 2008; Dellarocas 2003). These studies found that reviews matter: a one-star increase leads to a 5-9% increase in revenue. Yet, to the best of our knowledge, whether reviewer identity affects this relationship remains somewhat underexplored.

To investigate this question, we use a dataset of 1.2 million reviews. We find that reviewer identity has a significant effect. Platform managers should therefore consider highlighting reviewer credentials.

```
Online platforms have transformed how consumers make purchasing decisions. A
substantial literature links online reviews to sales (Chevalier and Mayzlin
2006; Dellarocas 2003; Forman et al. 2008), with estimates suggesting that a
one-star increase in average rating is associated with a 5-9% increase in
revenue. Yet this work treats a review largely as a rating. Whether the
identity of the reviewer changes how much a review moves sales remains
underexplored.

We address this question using a novel dataset of 1.2 million reviews. We find
that reviewer identity significantly affects the review-sales relationship.
This result has direct implications for platform design: managers should
consider highlighting reviewer credentials.
```

Things to check before this goes further:

- **Forman et al. (2008) undercuts the gap claim.** That paper (in ISR) studies reviewer identity disclosure and sales. Calling identity "underexplored" while citing it invites a reviewer to object. I'd move it out of the opening citation group and position your contribution against it explicitly, for example by saying what identity means in your setting or what you add beyond disclosure.
- **The 5-9% figure needs a source.** I couldn't tie it to the three citations, and Dellarocas (2003) is largely conceptual rather than an estimate of rating effects. Cite the specific paper(s) it comes from. I changed "leads to" to "is associated with" because the original reads as causal. Restore the causal wording only if the cited studies identify causally.
- **"Significant effect" is vague for this audience.** ISR readers will want the direction and rough magnitude in the second paragraph, plus a one-line identification strategy. Once you have the numbers, that sentence is worth expanding.
- **I cut the hedges.** "It is important to note," "to the best of our knowledge," and "somewhat" are gone. If you want a hedge on the gap claim, put it back as "to our knowledge" rather than "somewhat."
